How AI is changing pharma and biotech jobs for PhDs in 2026

AI is creating new roles in drug development and changing what employers expect from scientists. Here's what's actually happening, which jobs are growing, and how to position yourself.

If you're a PhD or postdoc looking at industry, you've probably seen two opposite headlines: AI will replace scientists, or AI is the hottest hiring area in pharma. The reality in 2026 sits in between, and it's mostly good news for scientists who learn to work alongside these tools.

What the hiring data says

Recruiters report that AI isn't eliminating technical jobs. In an October 2026 Fierce Biotech report, a vice president of science recruiting at Kelly said, "We're not seeing technical positions going away due to AI." Instead, hiring managers are "starting to look for individuals who have the AI knowledge and skills alongside the scientific or clinical discipline."

Large drugmakers are building dedicated AI teams. Public announcements tracked in 2026 include:

  • Sanofi expanding its Toronto AI hub, with 50 AI and machine learning roles planned by 2028.
  • Boehringer Ingelheim opening an AI and machine learning center in London in April 2026.
  • Eli Lilly and Nvidia launching a joint AI lab, with up to $1 billion committed over five years.
  • Roche and Genentech running a computational sciences center backed by thousands of GPUs.

At the same time, overall layoffs have cooled. BioSpace counted 2,568 affected employees in Q3 2026, the lowest quarterly total since it began tracking in 2024.

The job title "AI drug discovery scientist" was rare a few years ago. Recruiters now see it regularly.

Regulators are shaping the jobs too

In January 2026, the FDA and the European Medicines Agency released ten shared principles for using AI in drug development. They cover a risk-based approach, validation scaled to risk, a clearly defined context of use, strong data governance and ongoing monitoring of model performance.

For scientists, this matters beyond data science teams. Regulatory Affairs, Quality and Analytical Development groups now need people who understand how an AI model was built and validated, and who can explain it to an agency. That's a natural fit for PhDs who already think in terms of controls, validation and evidence.

You don't need to become a programmer

Most of these roles want scientific depth plus AI literacy, not a computer science degree. AI literacy means you can:

  1. Explain what a model does, what data trained it, and where it might fail.
  2. Judge whether a model's output makes scientific sense.
  3. Work with data in Python or R well enough to explore results yourself.
  4. Describe validation and limitations in plain language for non-experts.

How to position yourself in the next 90 days

  1. Audit what you already do. Image analysis, curve fitting, statistics, scripting and large datasets all count. Put them on your resume with the tools named.
  2. Learn one practical skill. A short course in Python for data analysis, or machine learning basics for scientists, is enough to start.
  3. Build a small project. Reanalyze a public dataset from your field and write up what you found. Link it from your LinkedIn profile.
  4. Use the right keywords. Postings use terms like machine learning, computational, data science, Python, model validation and AI. Mirror the ones that truly describe your work.
  5. Target hybrid roles. Look for titles that combine your science with data, such as computational chemist, translational data scientist or quantitative pharmacologist.

Resume example

Before: Analyzed microscopy images for cell morphology studies.

After: Built a Python image-analysis pipeline that automated morphology scoring for 10,000+ cells, replacing manual counts.

The bottom line

AI is raising the bar, not closing the door. Scientists who pair their domain knowledge with practical data skills are the people pharma and biotech are trying to hire. Start by showing the computational work you already do, and add one new skill at a time.
