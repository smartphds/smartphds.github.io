Management consulting for PhDs: how case interviews work

Consulting firms hire PhDs from every field, but almost all of them test you with a case interview first. Here's what happens in the room, what interviewers score, and how to prepare without a business degree.

Management consulting is one of the few industry paths open to PhDs from any discipline. A physicist, a historian and a neuroscientist can all apply for the same consultant role. The catch is the hiring process. Instead of asking about your thesis, the firm hands you a business problem you've never seen and watches how you solve it, live.

That's the case interview. It feels unfamiliar, but it rewards habits you already have from research: breaking a big question into parts, forming hypotheses, testing them with data and explaining what you found.

Do consulting firms really hire PhDs?

Yes, and the largest firms say so directly. McKinsey lists candidates with "another postgraduate degree (e.g., PhD, Post-doc, MD, JD)" as eligible for its advanced professional degree track, with full-time associate roles and summer associate internships. BCG runs a dedicated advanced degree recruiting track for PhDs, postdoctoral researchers, MDs and JDs, and says it offers "customized training programs for non-business background consultants."

Timing varies by firm and office. BCG, for example, notes that its U.S. offices do not offer internships for advanced degree candidates, and recruits them for full-time roles starting the following year. Check each firm's careers page for your country before you plan your year.

What the hiring process looks like

Details differ between firms, but the overall shape is similar:

  1. Application. A one- or two-page resume, not an academic CV. Lead with results and leadership, not publications.
  2. Online assessment (at some firms). McKinsey uses Solve, a gamified test of problem solving. McKinsey says there's "no need to prepare," so treat it as a sample of how you think rather than something to cram.
  3. Experience or fit interview. You talk through past accomplishments in detail. McKinsey calls this the Personal Experience Interview and suggests preparing two personal examples. BCG includes a skills interview and a behavioral portion.
  4. Case interviews. One or more rounds where you solve a business problem with an interviewer. Later rounds often add further cases with more senior interviewers.

What happens in a case interview

The interviewer describes a client situation in a few sentences and asks a question, such as why profits fell or whether to enter a new market. You then work toward a recommendation. A typical case moves through five steps:

  1. Clarify. Restate the problem and confirm the objective. What does the client want, by when, and how will success be measured?
  2. Structure. Take a moment to lay out how you'll break the problem down, then walk the interviewer through it.
  3. Analyze. Ask for data, read charts or tables, and do quick math out loud. BCG says candidates are expected to "perform quick calculations."
  4. Synthesize. Say what each finding means for the client's question, not only what the number is.
  5. Recommend. Close with a clear answer, the main reasons, the risks and the next steps.

There's rarely a single right answer. BCG says "what matters most is how you approach the problem and the quality of your reasoning," and Bain says it looks at "how you think." McKinsey adds that "there is not one logical solution," so you should tailor your approach rather than force a memorized framework onto every problem.

Common case types

Case typeTypical questionWhat it tests
ProfitabilityWhy have this company's profits dropped?Splitting a problem into revenue and costs, then finding the driver
Market entryShould the client launch in a new country or segment?Weighing market attractiveness, competition and the client's ability to win
Market sizingHow big is the market for a new product?Building a clear estimate from reasonable assumptions
GrowthHow can the client double revenue in five years?Generating and prioritizing options
New business or investmentIs opening this business a good idea?Judging costs, demand and risk to reach a yes or no

Bain's public practice cases show two of these: one asks whether opening a coffee shop is a good idea, the other why a fashion company's revenue has fallen for five years. Working through the official practice cases on McKinsey, BCG and Bain's websites is the best way to see the real format.

What interviewers are scoring

Firms describe their criteria in similar terms. BCG lists communication, logical thinking, business intuition and creativity. In practice, you're being judged on:

  • Structure. Is your breakdown logical, complete and without overlap?
  • Hypothesis-driven thinking. Do you say what you think is going on, then test it?
  • Comfort with numbers. Can you do mental math accurately and sense-check results?
  • Communication. Can a busy client follow you? Lead with the answer, then give reasons.
  • Judgment. Do you focus on what matters most and drop what doesn't?

Where PhDs shine, and where they stumble

PhDs often do well on structure and data interpretation. You've spent years reading figures critically and asking what's missing. The common stumbles are about style, not ability:

  • Too much precision. Consultants round numbers to keep moving. An answer that's roughly right in two minutes beats an exact one in ten.
  • Answer last. Academic writing builds to a conclusion. Consulting starts with it.
  • Waiting for complete data. You're expected to commit to a hypothesis early and revise it as you learn more.
  • Going deep too fast. Cover the whole problem first, then dig into the part that matters.

Example synthesis (illustrative)

Before: So we looked at revenue by product, and costs across the three plants, and there are several factors, and more data would help clarify the picture.

After: The profit drop comes mostly from rising costs at one plant, not from falling sales. I'd recommend auditing that plant's supplier contracts first, and checking whether the problem could spread to the other two.

How to prepare in six to eight weeks

  1. Learn basic business vocabulary. Revenue, fixed and variable costs, margin, market share and break-even are enough to start.
  2. Do the official practice cases. McKinsey, BCG and Bain all publish examples with suggested approaches.
  3. Practice live with partners. Cases are spoken, so reading alone isn't enough. Many universities have consulting clubs, and some welcome graduate students and postdocs.
  4. Drill mental math. Percentages, growth rates and large-number multiplication, a few minutes a day.
  5. Prepare your stories. Pick two or three examples of leading, persuading or overcoming setbacks from your research, and tell each in under two minutes.
  6. Translate your resume. Show impact, teamwork and leadership. Consulting firms read resumes much like any other industry employer.

A note on tools: McKinsey says it's fine to use AI to prepare, such as practicing questions, but not to generate answers during interviews or assessments.

The bottom line

Case interviews aren't a test of business knowledge. They're a test of structured thinking under time pressure, explained clearly. Your PhD already gave you most of the thinking. What you need to add is speed, a business vocabulary and the habit of leading with the answer.
