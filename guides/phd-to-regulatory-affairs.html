PhD to Regulatory Affairs: what the job really is

Regulatory Affairs is one of the most common non-lab roles for PhDs in pharma and biotech. Here's what the work looks like day to day, who it suits, and how to get your first role.

Every new medicine needs permission before it can be tested in people or sold. Regulatory Affairs, often shortened to RA, is the team that earns that permission. They plan what evidence the health authorities will need, assemble it into submissions, and answer the agencies' questions until the product is approved and stays approved.

If you enjoyed writing papers, defending your data and getting every detail right, RA may suit you better than another postdoc.

What Regulatory Affairs actually does

  • Plans the regulatory strategy. Which studies are needed, in which order, and for which countries.
  • Writes and assembles submissions. In the US these include the IND to start clinical trials and the NDA or BLA to request approval. Most regions use the same structure, the Common Technical Document, or CTD.
  • Talks to the agencies. RA prepares meeting requests and briefing documents and coordinates answers when the FDA or another authority sends questions.
  • Keeps products compliant after approval. Manufacturing changes, new safety information and label updates all need regulatory review, and often a new filing.

A typical week

Expect less time at a bench and more time in documents and meetings. A week might include reviewing a stability report before it goes into a submission, writing a response to an agency question, joining a cross-functional meeting with manufacturing and clinical teams, and checking whether a proposed process change needs to be reported.

The rhythm follows submission deadlines. Quiet weeks of review are punctuated by intense weeks before a filing goes out.

Why PhDs do well in RA

  • You can read and judge data. RA has to decide whether the evidence actually supports the claim.
  • You can write a clear scientific argument. A submission is a long, structured argument with references.
  • You're used to peer review. Answering agency questions feels a lot like answering reviewers, with higher stakes.
  • You can learn a technical field fast. Regulations and guidelines are dense, but they're far less dense than a thesis literature review.

How to get your first RA role

  1. Learn the vocabulary. Read the FDA's overview of the IND and NDA process and skim the ICH guidelines that match your science, for example ICH Q-series guidelines for quality and manufacturing topics.
  2. Translate your research into regulatory language. "Optimized an HPLC method" becomes "developed and qualified an analytical method suitable for release testing."
  3. Target entry titles. Look for Regulatory Affairs Associate, Regulatory Specialist, Regulatory Writer or Regulatory CMC Scientist.
  4. Consider a credential later, not first. RAPS offers the Regulatory Affairs Certification, RAC. It carries weight, but most people earn it after a year or two in the field. It isn't required to get hired.
  5. Talk to people in the role. Short informational calls with RA professionals will teach you more than any course. They also lead to referrals.

Resume example

Before: Characterized protein stability under different buffer conditions.

After: Designed and ran stability studies across 12 formulation conditions, defining storage conditions used by two downstream projects.

Is RA right for you?

It suits you if you like precision, structured writing and seeing a project through to a clear outcome. It may not suit you if you want to keep running experiments every day or prefer open-ended exploration over deadlines.
