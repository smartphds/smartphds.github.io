Should you do a postdoc before industry?

For some industry roles a postdoc is a real advantage. For many others it costs you years without improving your odds. Here's how to tell which one you're facing.

Many PhDs take a postdoc by default. It feels safe, it keeps academic doors open, and it buys time to decide. But if you already suspect you're heading to industry, the default deserves a closer look, because a postdoc is a choice with real costs as well as real benefits.

What the data says

Postdocs are still a common next step. According to the National Science Foundation's survey of doctorate recipients from U.S. universities in 2024, 39% of new PhDs with definite U.S. plans were heading into postdoc positions, and the postdoc share rose between 2023 and 2024 in most science and engineering fields, most of all in the physical sciences, biological and biomedical sciences, and computer science. The same report found that the share of non-postdoc jobs going to industry has declined in all science and engineering fields since at least 2022.

So more graduates are choosing postdocs while the industry job market is tighter. That makes the decision more important, not less.

The best long-term evidence on cost comes from biomedicine. A study by economists Shulamit Kahn and Donna Ginther in Nature Biotechnology, using decades of NSF survey data, found a substantial and cumulative pay penalty for starting a career in a postdoc. On average, former postdocs did not earn more after the postdoc than they would have earned by skipping it, and the authors estimated that a postdoc cost roughly three years of salary over the first 15 years of a career.

When a postdoc helps an industry career

A postdoc is worth it when it gives you something specific that your target employers ask for and that you can't get any faster another way. Common cases:

  • Discovery and research scientist roles. Some research positions in pharma and biotech, especially at larger companies, list postdoctoral experience as required or preferred. Check ten real postings for your target title before you decide.
  • A skill gap you can close. If your PhD lacked a technique industry values, such as single-cell methods, cryo-EM, mass spectrometry or machine learning, a short, focused postdoc can fill it.
  • A field switch. Moving from, say, chemistry into immunology is easier with a postdoc that proves you can work in the new area.
  • An industry postdoc. A handful of companies, including Novartis, Genentech and AbbVie, run postdoc programs inside their own R&D groups. You do real research, learn how a company works, and build a network with people who hire.

When you can probably skip it

For many industry paths, employers hire PhDs directly and value relevant skills over extra years of research. A postdoc rarely moves the needle for:

  • Data science, analytics and machine learning roles, where portfolios and coding skills matter more.
  • Management consulting, where firms recruit PhDs straight from graduate school through case interviews.
  • Product management, quant finance and tech roles.
  • Regulatory affairs, quality, medical writing and medical affairs.
  • Patent work, science policy and science communication.
  • Most industry paths for social science and humanities PhDs, such as user research, policy analysis and market research.

For these roles, a year spent learning the tools, building a project and networking usually beats a year at the bench.

Five questions to ask before you accept

  1. What job do I want after this? Name the title. If you can't, a postdoc is a costly way to find out. Informational interviews are cheaper.
  2. Do postings for that job ask for a postdoc? If most don't, you probably don't need one.
  3. What will I have at the end that I don't have now? A new technique, a new field, or industry contacts are good answers. "More papers" alone usually isn't.
  4. Will my advisor support an industry plan? Ask directly about time for networking, courses and internships.
  5. How long, exactly? Set a limit before you start, such as two years, and a date to begin applying.

If you're already a postdoc

You haven't lost anything by choosing a postdoc, but the cost grows the longer you stay without a plan. Make the most of the time you have:

  • Pick one or two target roles and start applying six to nine months before your funding ends.
  • Turn your postdoc into industry language. Projects led, people trained, collaborations managed and budgets handled are all management experience.
  • Add one industry skill a quarter, like Python, GMP basics or project management, depending on your target role.
  • Talk to former postdocs from your lab or institute who now work in industry. They know which skills got them hired.

Before: Postdoctoral fellow studying T-cell signaling.

The bottom line

Do a postdoc when it's the clearest route to a specific industry job, ideally one inside a company. Skip it when your target role hires PhDs directly, and spend the time on skills, projects and contacts instead. Either way, decide on purpose, not by default.
