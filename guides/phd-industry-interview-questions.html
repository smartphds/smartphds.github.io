The 5 interview questions every PhD gets in industry interviews

Whether you're interviewing for data science, consulting, biotech or policy, the same five questions come up again and again. Here's what each one is really asking and how to answer it well.

Industry interviews vary a lot by field. A data science loop may include a coding exercise, a pharma interview a job talk, a consulting process a case. But underneath the technical rounds, PhDs and postdocs hear a remarkably consistent set of questions. University career offices that coach researchers, including UCSF, Johns Hopkins and Oxford Brookes, publish lists of industry interview questions for PhDs, and the same themes appear on all of them.

These questions sound simple. That's the trap. Most PhDs answer them the way an academic committee would want, and the answer lands badly with a hiring manager. Prepare these five, and you'll walk into most interviews with half the conversation already planned.

1. "Tell me about yourself"

This is usually the opener, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. The interviewer isn't asking for your life story or a tour of your CV. They want to know who you are professionally and why you're sitting in front of them for this role.

UCSF's career office suggests responding with "a summary of your academic training, professional skills and experience and interest in the position." A simple way to structure that in about 90 seconds:

  1. Present. What you do now, in one sentence a non-specialist can follow.
  2. Past. Two or three strengths or results that matter for this job.
  3. Future. Why this role is the natural next step.

Start with your PhD only if it's the strongest link to the role. Otherwise lead with the skill they're hiring for.

2. "Tell me about your research"

This one catches PhDs off guard because it seems like home ground. The risk is giving the conference version: too long, too technical, and focused on findings the company will never use.

Johns Hopkins' career office notes that employers are "often more interested in the techniques and skills you acquired" than in "the specifics of your research," and advises avoiding "too much scientific jargon." Oxford Brookes lists a sharper follow-up you should expect: "How is your research related to the work we do?"

  • Give the problem first. Why did the question matter, in plain words?
  • Then your approach. The methods and tools you used, especially ones in the job posting.
  • Then the result. What changed because of your work, and who used it.
  • Bridge to the job. One sentence linking your skills to their work.

Aim for two minutes, then stop and let them ask for more. If they want depth, they'll ask. A humanities or social science PhD can use the same structure: the question, your sources or data, what you found and the skills it took.

Example answer (illustrative)

Before: My thesis investigated the role of a regulatory protein in mitochondrial dynamics in a specific cell model, using a range of imaging and biochemical approaches, and we found several unexpected interactions.

After: I studied why some cells lose energy production under stress. I built an automated imaging pipeline that cut analysis time for our lab from days to hours, and I'd bring that same mix of lab work and data analysis to your assay development team.

3. "Why are you leaving academia?"

Some version of this appears on nearly every PhD interview list: "Why did you decide to leave academia?" or "Why are you choosing to leave academe?" Interviewers want to know two things. Are you running toward this job, or just away from a tough academic market? And will you be happy in a setting with deadlines, teams and commercial goals?

Answer in a positive and confident way. Focus on what draws you to industry, not what frustrated you about academia. Good answers often mention:

  • Wanting to see your work used by patients, customers or decision makers.
  • Enjoying collaboration across disciplines more than working alone.
  • Liking faster cycles and clearer goals.
  • A specific experience, such as a collaboration or internship, that showed you this path fits.

Avoid complaining about funding, supervisors or the job market. Even if those were real factors, they don't tell the employer why you'll thrive with them. Expect a related question too: Oxford Brookes lists "What do you see as the main differences between academic research and working in a more commercial environment?"

4. "Tell me about a time when..."

This is the behavioral question, and you'll usually get several. Common versions ask about a setback, a conflict, a tight deadline, or working in a team. UCSF's list includes "Tell me about a time that you failed or faced a significant professional setback. How did you handle it?"

Some employers build their whole process around these questions. Amazon describes its interviews as "distinctly behavioral-based" and calls the STAR method ("situation, task, action, and result") the cornerstone of them.

StepWhat to sayCommon PhD mistake
SituationThe context in one or two sentencesToo much scientific background
TaskWhat you were responsible forDescribing the lab's goal, not yours
ActionThe specific steps you tookSaying "we" throughout
ResultWhat changed, and what you learnedStopping before the outcome

Amazon suggests each story should last about two to three minutes and use "I" statements to make your own contribution clear. That advice works almost everywhere. Prepare five or six stories from your PhD or postdoc that cover leadership, conflict, failure, teamwork, influence without authority and delivering under pressure. One good story can often answer several different questions.

5. "Why do you want to work here?"

Interviewers ask this to test whether you've done your homework and whether you actually want this job rather than any job. Oxford Brookes lists a blunter version: "What do you know about our organisation?"

A strong answer has three parts:

  1. Something specific about them. A product, pipeline, client area, mission or recent announcement, ideally something you read about in the past week.
  2. Why it matters to you. Connect it to your interests or past work.
  3. What you'd contribute. One skill or experience that fits what they need.

Generic praise ("you're a leader in the field") doesn't count. If your answer would work for a competitor, it's too general.

Bonus: "Do you have any questions for us?"

It almost always comes at the end, and Johns Hopkins is clear that "the right answer is yes." Prepare three or four questions about the role, the team and how success is measured. UCSF suggests asking "What qualities are you looking for in the successful candidate?" Amazon recommends "thoughtful questions about the role, team dynamics, and growth opportunities." Leave salary and benefits for later in the process unless the interviewer raises them.

How to prepare in one week

  • Day 1–2: Write your 90-second introduction and two-minute research summary. Read them aloud and cut anything a non-specialist wouldn't follow.
  • Day 3–4: Draft five or six STAR stories with clear results.
  • Day 5: Research the company and write your "why us" answer and your questions for them.
  • Day 6–7: Do at least one mock interview with a friend, mentor or your career office, and record yourself if you can.

The bottom line

Industry interviewers already assume you're smart. These five questions test something else: whether you can explain your value clearly, briefly and in their terms. The good news is that they're predictable, so you can prepare them now and reuse them in every interview, whatever field you're moving into.
